Product Design · XING · 2017

Restructuring XING Settings Around How Users Think, Not How the Product Is Built

The problem

XING's settings had been built the way most settings get built at fast-growing products: each feature team owned the settings relevant to their area, and added them where it made sense at the time. Over years of growth, this had produced a settings experience organized around XING's internal product structure — not around the mental models of the people trying to use it.

Privacy controls were split across multiple sections. Notification preferences lived in three different places. Users who wanted to do something simple had to hunt.

My role

Senior Interaction Designer. I led user research, information architecture, interaction design, and prototyping.

Research

We started with a task-based usability study on the existing settings, asking users to complete common tasks: update their password, change who can see their profile activity, cancel a subscription. The failure rates told a consistent story — users were lost not because the settings didn't exist, but because they didn't know where to look.

We followed that with a card sort to understand how users naturally grouped settings concepts, which surfaced clear clusters that didn't match the existing structure at all.

Information architecture

The card sort led to four top-level categories, each aligned to a user goal rather than a product area:

  • User account — personal information, login data, active logged-in devices
  • Privacy — profile visibility, activity visibility, data and tracking controls
  • Notifications — email, in-app, recommendations and tips
  • Purchases — all subscriptions, upgrades, and invoices in one place

Design approach

Mobile-first throughout. The majority of XING's active users accessed settings on mobile, but the existing settings were clearly designed desktop-first and adapted down. We inverted the process: designed for touch interaction, progressively enhanced to desktop.

Prototyped in high fidelity and tested two rounds with users before final handoff, validating that the new structure reduced task completion time and eliminated the most common navigation dead-ends.
